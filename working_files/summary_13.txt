“Digital Colonialism”: U.S. Demands To Access Africans’ Data Raise Privacy, Sovereignty Concerns
============================================================

The U.S. government is conditioning billions in global health aid on access to sensitive citizen data, sparking international concerns over digital sovereignty, privacy risks, and potential data exploitation.

### Key Points
- The "America First Global Health Strategy" requires recipient nations to grant the U.S. direct, real-time access to national health systems and electronic medical records.
- Uganda, Kenya, and Liberia are among the more than 30 countries facing these transactional agreements in exchange for funding for HIV, malaria, and pandemic response.
- Critics and privacy experts warn that the deals lack standard protections, potentially allowing for the re-identification of anonymized data or commercial exploitation by private companies.
- Several nations, including Zambia, Zimbabwe, and Ghana, have rejected initial proposals due to concerns over the invasive nature of the data-sharing requirements.
- The State Department has declined to publicly release the full agreements, citing the need to protect ongoing negotiations, leading to lawsuits from advocacy groups like Public Citizen.

### Why it Matters
This shift toward a transactional aid model risks undermining long-standing international health partnerships and eroding trust in U.S.-led global health security efforts. By prioritizing data access over traditional humanitarian support, the policy may inadvertently weaken pandemic surveillance and leave vulnerable populations exposed to significant privacy breaches.
