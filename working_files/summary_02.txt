Why mathematicians are hiding research results in zero-knowledge proofs
============================================================

Google researchers claimed in March 2026 that quantum computers could soon break common encryption, attempting to prove the finding using a controversial, secret-keeping zero-knowledge proof method.

### Key Points
- Google researchers asserted that 1,175 logical qubits and two million Toffoli gates could compromise current elliptic curve cryptography.
- The company utilized a "zero-knowledge proof" to claim the discovery without revealing the underlying algorithm, citing security risks.
- Independent cryptologist André Schrottenloher successfully reproduced and improved upon Google’s quantum algorithm within 63 days.
- An online community using AI-based systems further optimized the process, nearly halving the required number of Toffoli gates.
- Google’s initial zero-knowledge proof was found to be flawed, allowing for the validation of false claims before a correction was issued.

### Why it Matters
This event highlights the tension between corporate secrecy and the open-source nature of scientific progress in the field of quantum computing. The failure of Google's obfuscation strategy suggests that transparency remains the most effective way to verify and advance critical breakthroughs in digital security.
